## Every article on this site starts somewhere in the middle of a debt problem

A collection letter. A court date. A charge-off sitting on a credit report. This one starts earlier than all of that: the moment when the car needs a repair you can't cover, or rent is three days ahead of payday, and a storefront window or a phone app offers to bridge the gap right now.

That product is a payday loan. It's worth understanding before you accept one — not because borrowing a few hundred dollars in an emergency is a character flaw, but because the fee is designed to be misread. This article covers how the product actually works, what the fee translates to on an annual basis, why so many loans get reborrowed instead of repaid, where the product is restricted, and what the real exits look like if you're already in.

## What a payday loan actually is

A payday loan is a small-dollar, short-term advance — typically a few hundred dollars — due in full, with the fee, on your next payday. In practice, you either write a post-dated check for the full amount or authorize the lender to debit your bank account on the due date. Approval is fast, and it usually doesn't involve a credit check, which is exactly what the advertising leads with.

"No credit check" cuts both ways. It means you can get the loan with damaged or thin credit, and fast. It also means the lender isn't checking whether the loan fits your budget. The product is built around your next paycheck, not around whether that paycheck is already spoken for.

A payday loan is a pledge of income you haven't earned yet — you're promising tomorrow's paycheck, before you know what tomorrow will actually cost. That's not a verdict on anyone who takes one; emergencies don't schedule themselves around paydays. But it is an accurate description of the paperwork, and there's an old proverb about exactly that kind of pledge: "Be not one of those who give pledges, who put up security for debts. If you have nothing with which to pay, why should your bed be taken from under you?" (Proverbs 22:26–27, ESV).

## What the fee really costs

Payday loans are priced as a flat fee per amount borrowed, not as an interest rate — and that presentation is doing a lot of work. The CFPB's own rulemaking (12 C.F.R. § 1041.1) describes a typical fee of 15% of the transaction amount per two-week period — on a $500 loan, $75 due alongside the principal in two weeks — and cites a commonly reported APR of about 391% for a two-week payday loan.

The gap between "$75" and "391%" is the whole trick. APR annualizes a cost over a year; a payday loan runs two weeks. A 15% charge would be unremarkable spread across a year-long loan. Compressed into 14 days and annualized, it becomes the ~391% APR in the CFPB's cited example — which is why the sticker fee reads as cheap and the annualized cost does not.

One honest caveat before you take that number anywhere:

- **The ~391% figure is the CFPB's cited example, not a universal truth.** Fees and allowed loan structures vary by state, and some states cap them. The honest framing is "the CFPB cites about 391% for a typical two-week loan" — not "payday loans cost 391%."

## The debt trap

Here is the number that explains the industry: in CFPB research, more than 80% of payday loans are rolled over or reborrowed within 30 days.

The mechanics are simple. The loan comes due in full on your next payday — which is, by definition, before your budget has recovered from whatever emergency sent you to the lender in the first place. So borrowers pay the fee again to extend the loan, or take out a second loan to pay off the first. Each cycle charges another fee and retires no principal. The CFPB's 2017 rulemaking described exactly this pattern, arguing that repeat reborrowing turns a short-term fix into a longer-term debt trap.

One caution on that: rollover limits are state-specific. In some states a lender can't extend a loan indefinitely; in others it can. The 80% figure describes observed borrower behavior, not a legal rule — the trap is built into the product's design (one lump sum, due before you're paid again), not into a statute.

## Where payday loans are banned or capped

For most borrowers there is no federal cap on payday loan fees. The enforceable limits live at two other levels:

- **State law.** States regulate or prohibit payday lending directly — a number of states cap rates or ban the product outright, while others allow it with limits on loan size, fees, or rollovers. Which camp your state falls in shapes most of what happens next.
- **The Military Lending Act.** Covered consumer credit to servicemembers and their dependents is capped at a 36% military annual percentage rate (MAPR) under 10 U.S.C. § 987, and payday-style lending to covered borrowers is restricted. If that's you or a covered dependent, stricter rules apply to you than to your civilian neighbors.

A note on the federal rule you may have read about: in 2017 the CFPB finalized a rule that would have required payday lenders to verify a borrower's ability to repay. A federal court vacated the rule's underwriting provisions in 2022, and the CFPB has since confirmed those provisions will never take effect. The rule's remaining payment provisions took effect in March 2025, but the CFPB has said it will not prioritize enforcement of the penalties or fines associated with them, and it is considering narrowing the rule's scope. The practical version: don't count on a federal rule to protect you here. State law and the military cap are where the real limits live today.

## How to get out

None of these steps requires a company that promises to make your loans disappear. In fact, that promise is the first thing to refuse — the FTC warns specifically against payday-debt-relief offers that charge upfront fees. If a company wants money from you today in exchange for freedom from your payday loans later, walk away.

What actually helps:

- **Nonprofit credit counseling.** A counselor at a nonprofit agency can help you build a budget and a payoff plan, and the Department of Justice maintains the [list of approved agencies](https://www.justice.gov/ust/list-credit-counseling-agencies-approved-pursuant-11-usc-111). This is the standard first call when any debt — payday or otherwise — has outgrown your budget.
- **Earned wage access, if your employer offers it.** These products let you draw pay you've already earned before payday. Some are employer-integrated; others charge fees that add up. The CFPB has issued interpretive guidance on when these products count as consumer credit under Regulation Z — the regulatory ground is still settling. EWA speaks to the same need a payday loan gets borrowed for — cash you've earned but don't have yet — but read the fee schedule before you rely on one.
- **Small-dollar alternatives.** Credit unions and state-licensed lenders offer small-dollar alternatives to a two-week, due-in-full payday loan.

And the one thing not to do: don't stack. Taking a second payday loan to pay the first isn't an exit — it's the engine of the trap described above.

## If a payday loan has already gone wrong

If the due date arrives and the money isn't there, the loan typically gets rolled over or reborrowed — the trap described above — and an unpaid payday debt can eventually be placed with or sold to a collector.

Once a collector owns or services the debt, federal collection law applies. [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/) covers the conduct rules, and the first move with any collector is making it prove the debt — [Debt Validation Letters: What They Are, When to Send One, and What They Actually Do](/resources/article/debt-validation-letter/) walks through how.

From there, an unpaid payday loan follows the same enforcement chain as any other unsecured debt. The account can be charged off — an accounting event, not a forgiveness of what you owe ([What Does "Charged Off" Mean on Your Credit Report? (And Do You Still Owe the Debt?)](/resources/article/what-does-charge-off-mean/)). The lender or a debt buyer can sue ([Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/)). With a judgment in hand, a creditor can garnish wages ([Wage Garnishment: How Much Can They Take From Your Paycheck?](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/)) or levy a bank account, and in some cases a judgment becomes a lien that clouds the title to a home ([Judgment Lien on Your House: What It Means and What a Creditor Can Actually Do](/resources/article/judgment-lien-on-your-house/)). Bankruptcy is a separate legal path with its own rules and costs — not a step to take over a $500 loan without qualified advice.

The earlier you interrupt that chain, the fewer of those doors open. If a payday loan has already rolled over more than once, the credit-counseling route in the previous section is the place to start.

## The short version

- A payday loan is a small-dollar advance due in full, with a flat fee, on your next payday — secured by a post-dated check or a debit authorization. "No credit check" also means no affordability check.
- The CFPB's rulemaking describes a typical fee of 15% per two-week period and cites a commonly reported APR of about 391% for a two-week loan — an attributed example, not a universal number. Fees vary by state.
- More than 80% of payday loans are rolled over or reborrowed within 30 days (CFPB research). Each cycle charges another fee and retires no principal.
- No federal cap protects most borrowers: the 2017 CFPB rule's ability-to-repay provisions were vacated and will never take effect. State bans and caps, and the Military Lending Act's 36% cap for servicemembers, are the enforceable limits today.
- Real exits: nonprofit credit counseling (DOJ-approved list), earned wage access where your employer offers it (read the fees), and small-dollar credit-union or state-licensed loans. Refuse upfront-fee "payday relief" offers.
- If the loan has already gone wrong: make any collector validate the debt, know the charge-off → lawsuit → garnishment sequence, and interrupt the chain early.
