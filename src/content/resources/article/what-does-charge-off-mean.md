## What a charge-off actually is (and what it isn't)

Few entries on a credit report cause as much confusion as "charged off." In everyday language, *written off* sounds final — forgiven, forgotten, over. On a lender's books, it means something narrower.

So what does charge off mean, exactly? A charge-off is an **accounting action**. When an account goes unpaid for long enough, the original creditor writes it off as a loss on its own books and stops treating it as an active, earning account. It's a decision about the lender's ledger. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-does-charge-off-mean-en-318/))

What it isn't: forgiveness. The debt generally still exists, and the obligation generally remains. Collectors can still contact you about it. It can still be sued on within your state's statute of limitations. And in specific situations it can even reach your taxes. The rest of this article unpacks each of those — because the gap between what "charged off" sounds like and what it actually means is where expensive mistakes happen.

## When it happens — and what it looks like on your credit report

For credit card accounts, federal banking guidance treats an account as generally charged off around **180 days of nonpayment**. ([OCC Bulletin 2000-20](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2000/bulletin-2000-20.html)) Treat that as the general pattern, not a rule with your name on it — exact timing varies by creditor, account type, and the terms of the agreement.

On your credit report, the account typically shows a status like "charged off," and it's negative information. If the debt was placed with a collection agency or sold, a separate collection entry may appear alongside the original account. Both are governed by the reporting clock described below.

## Do you still owe it? Who can collect a charged-off debt

Can a debt collector collect a charged-off debt? In general, yes — you still owe it. The write-off changed the lender's books, not the underlying obligation. Who might come collecting on it:

- **The original creditor**, collecting its own debt.
- **A collection agency it hired** — the debt stays the creditor's; the agency collects on its behalf.
- **A debt buyer that purchased it** — the buyer now owns the debt.

Which conduct rules apply depends on who's doing the collecting. Under the FDCPA, a buyer that acquired the debt after default is generally a "debt collector" subject to the law's conduct rules; a creditor collecting its own debts generally isn't. (15 U.S.C. §1692a(6)) The day-to-day rules — call practices, what a collector must tell you, how to dispute — are in [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/).

One thing not to assume from the credit report: **whether the debt was sold or assigned**. The two paths differ in who owns the debt and who you'd ultimately be paying, and the report doesn't always make it clear. Ask the collector directly and verify — don't deduce it from the entry.

On interest and fees: whether they keep running after charge-off depends on the account agreement and state law, so don't assume they stopped. On the collection side, the FDCPA bars collectors from collecting any amount not permitted by the agreement or by law. (15 U.S.C. §1692f)

There's a piece of old wisdom that fits here. Psalm 37:21 draws a line between the person who "borrows and does not pay back" and the one who is "generous and gives" (ESV). The contrast is about a settled direction of life, not a verdict on anyone in a hard season — people arrive at charge-offs through job loss, illness, and plain bad luck, and none of that is the point of this article. The verse and this article land in the same place: an accounting entry doesn't dissolve what's owed, so the honest path is to face the obligation — find out what's actually owed, verify it, and resolve it lawfully — rather than treating the write-off as an ending.

## The reporting clock: seven years plus 180 days, and why a sale doesn't restart it

A charged-off account is negative information, and the FCRA limits how long it may be reported: charged-off and placed-for-collection accounts may be reported for up to **seven years plus 180 days** — and the clock runs from the date the account first went delinquent (the delinquency that led to the charge-off), **not** from the charge-off date. (15 U.S.C. §1681c(a)(4), (c)(1))

Two practical consequences:

- **The charge-off date on the report isn't the start of the clock.** If the account went delinquent months before the charge-off posted, that earlier date is what counts — which can mean less time on your report than you feared.
- **Selling doesn't re-age it.** The FCRA fixes the commencing date of the reporting period; a sale or transfer to a collector doesn't restart it. (15 U.S.C. §1681c(c)(1))

Don't confuse this with the other clock — the state-law **statute of limitations** on lawsuits, which a payment can sometimes affect. That clock is covered in [Time-Barred Debt: How Long Can a Debt Collector Come After You?](/resources/article/time-barred-debt/). Reporting and lawsuits run on separate tracks; keep them separate in your head, because mixing them up is how people end up either overestimating what a collector can still do or underestimating it.

What paying changes: payment or settlement updates the entry's status — "paid charge-off," "settled charge-off" — but the item itself stays on the report until the clock runs out. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-your-credit-report-en-206/)) What paying doesn't come with: a score prediction. There's no reliable published figure for how much any given entry moves a credit score, and anyone quoting you a precise number is guessing.

## What can happen next: collection, lawsuits, and the separate tax clock

**Collection continues.** A charged-off debt is still collectible, so calls and letters can keep coming. The conduct rules in the FDCPA post above apply the whole time.

**Lawsuits can follow.** Within the applicable statute of limitations, a charged-off debt can be sued on. If a judgment results, the post-judgment tools can come into play — wage garnishment (federal law caps what can be taken for most consumer debts) and, depending on your state's procedures, a levy against a bank account. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs)) The lawsuit process — summons, response deadline, default judgments — is in [Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/), and the garnishment rules are in [our wage garnishment guide](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/).

**The tax clock is separate.** A charge-off by itself is not a cancellation of debt and doesn't by itself generate a 1099-C. But settling a charged-off debt for less than the full balance can: forgiven amounts are generally treated as taxable income, and creditors generally must report cancellations of $600 or more. The rules also include a 36-month nonpayment test under which a creditor may treat debt as discharged — it applies only to debt discharged after December 31, 2018, it's conditional, the conditions matter, and it isn't a certainty for any particular account. Bankruptcy discharge is excluded from this treatment. (26 U.S.C. §6050P; [IRS Publication 4681](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p4681)) If a 1099-C arrives or you're weighing a settlement, that's a question for a tax professional — not a guess.

## First steps: verify the debt, request validation, pull your free reports

- **Pull your free credit reports.** They show the entry, the dates, and often who currently holds or is collecting the debt. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))
- **Request validation in writing, within 30 days of first contact.** If you dispute a debt in writing within that window, the collector must stop collection until it verifies the debt. (15 U.S.C. §1692g(b)) The mechanics are covered in the FDCPA post above.
- **Find out who holds the debt.** Original creditor, agency, or buyer — and whether it was sold or assigned. Ask the collector; the report alone doesn't settle it.
- **Dispute what's wrong — and only that.** Disputes exist for information that's inaccurate, incomplete, or can't be verified. They're not a removal strategy for accurate entries, and anyone promising guaranteed deletion of a correct charge-off for a fee is selling something the process doesn't deliver. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-your-credit-report-en-206/))

A charge-off is a serious entry with real consequences — but it sits inside a known process with defined clocks and defined rights. Facing it with facts beats assuming the debt vanished, and beats assuming nothing can be done, too.

## The short version

- A **charge-off is an accounting write-off** on the creditor's books — not forgiveness. The obligation generally remains. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-does-charge-off-mean-en-318/))
- Credit card accounts are generally charged off around **180 days of nonpayment** under federal banking guidance; exact timing varies. ([OCC Bulletin 2000-20](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2000/bulletin-2000-20.html))
- Charged-off accounts may be reported for up to **7 years plus 180 days from the first delinquency** — not the charge-off date — and selling the debt doesn't restart the clock. (15 U.S.C. §1681c(a)(4), (c)(1))
- The original creditor, a hired collection agency, or a debt buyer can all collect; a post-default buyer is generally a **"debt collector" under the FDCPA**. (15 U.S.C. §1692a(6))
- Paying or settling **updates the status but doesn't remove the entry**; no reliable score-impact figure exists, so beware of anyone quoting one. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-long-does-negative-information-remain-on-your-credit-report-en-206/))
- A charged-off debt can still be **sued on within the statute of limitations**; a judgment can enable wage garnishment or a bank levy. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))
- A charge-off alone doesn't trigger a **1099-C**, but settling for less than the balance can; the rules are conditional, and bankruptcy discharge is excluded. (26 U.S.C. §6050P; [IRS Publication 4681](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p4681))
- **First steps:** pull your free reports; request validation in writing within 30 days of first contact (15 U.S.C. §1692g(b)); find out who holds the debt; dispute only inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information.
