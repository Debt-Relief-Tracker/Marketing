## A paycheck, and a piece of paper nobody warned you about

Most people first hear the word "garnishment" when it's already happening — a chunk of their paycheck is missing, and an HR person hands them paperwork they've never seen. It feels like it came out of nowhere.

It usually didn't. For most consumer debts, garnishment is the **last step in a legal process**, not the first: a creditor sues, wins a judgment, and only then can it reach into wages. That means there are usually warning signs along the way — a summons, a lawsuit, a judgment — and federal law also puts a hard ceiling on how much can be taken. Understanding both things puts you back on solid ground. ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

## What wage garnishment is — and why a court order usually comes first

Wage garnishment is a legal procedure where a portion of your earnings is withheld to pay a debt, and it's typically handled through your employer.

For most consumer debts — credit cards, medical bills, personal loans — the creditor **usually must sue you and win a judgment first**. No judgment, generally no garnishment. ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

That process matters because it creates real decision points along the way. If you're contacted about a debt, responding keeps you in the process: someone who ignores a summons can end up with a **default judgment** against them, and a default judgment is exactly what enables a garnishment later. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))

One important exception: the federal government can garnish wages for certain federal debts — including defaulted federal student loans — **without a court order**, through a process called administrative wage garnishment. More on that below.

## The federal cap: 25% or the minimum-wage floor, whichever is less

For most consumer-debt garnishments, federal law (the Consumer Credit Protection Act, or CCPA) caps what can be taken from each paycheck at the **lesser** of:

- **25% of your disposable earnings** for the week, or
- the amount **by which** your weekly disposable earnings exceed **30 times the federal minimum wage** — $7.25 × 30 = **$217.50/week**. (15 U.S.C. §1673; [DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

Because the law takes the *lesser* of the two, that second formula acts as a floor of protection for lower-paid workers: if it leaves more of your paycheck untouched, it wins.

**What "disposable earnings" means here:** your pay after **legally required deductions** — federal tax, Social Security, and the like. It does **not** mean pay after voluntary deductions like a 401(k) contribution. If you contribute heavily to retirement while facing garnishment, the garnishment math doesn't treat that money as shielded. (15 U.S.C. §1672(b); [DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

## Different debts, different rules

Not every debt garnishes the same way, and a few categories have their own rules entirely:

- **Child support and alimony** have their own, much higher caps — support orders can reach **50–65% of disposable pay** depending on circumstances, outside the 25% consumer-debt limit. (15 U.S.C. §1673(b); [DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))
- **Federal debts** — including defaulted federal student loans — can be garnished **without a court order** through administrative wage garnishment, generally capped at about **15% of disposable pay**. (31 CFR §285.11; 20 U.S.C. §1095a)
- **Ordinary consumer debts** follow the 25%/minimum-wage-floor framework above, and only after a court judgment.

The practical takeaway: don't assume the 25% rule applies to your situation. The debt type changes the math.

## States can add protection beyond the federal cap

The federal cap is a ceiling on garnishment, not a floor — states can't allow **more** than federal law permits, but they can and often do allow **less**. Some states shield wages from consumer-debt garnishment almost entirely. ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

Because state rules vary widely, this article stays at the federal level. Check your own state's law — or ask a local legal aid office — to find out what applies where you live.

## Your job is protected — up to a point

A common fear: can I be fired over this? Federal law says an employer **can't fire you because of a single garnishment**. (15 U.S.C. §1674; [DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa))

Be aware of the limit, though: that protection covers **one** garnishment. An employer can fire you over two or more. It's a real boundary in the law, and it's better to know it than to be surprised by it.

## Before it happens: the earlier steps where you still have options

Because garnishment generally follows a judgment, the earlier steps of the process are where your options are widest. A few things worth knowing, generally:

- A lawsuit starts with a **summons and complaint** — the papers that officially tell you you're being sued. Taking those seriously and responding (or getting legal help to respond) is what matters most.
- **Judgments** are what enable garnishment. Responding is how you avoid a default judgment — the step that opens the door to garnishment.
- If a garnishment is already in place or a lawsuit is underway, options such as objections or exemption claims exist but **vary by state and court**. This article isn't legal advice, and it can't tell you which applies to you — a **legal aid office or a licensed consumer attorney in your state** can.

What's worth saying plainly: garnishment is a legal process with real structure to it, not something that usually arrives out of the blue. Knowing the sequence — debt → lawsuit → judgment → garnishment — and knowing the federal caps exist means facing the process with facts instead of fear. ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/30-cppa); [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))

## The mercy side: what the law protects

Proverbs 22:7 (CSB) says, "The rich rule over the poor, and the borrower is a slave to the lender." Garnishment is that verse made concrete — a lender's claim reaching into a borrower's paycheck. The limits described above are the other side of the same truth: a legal structure that says even a valid claim has boundaries, and a floor federal law puts under a worker's paycheck.

## The short version

- For most consumer debts, garnishment requires a **court judgment first** — no judgment, generally no garnishment.
- The federal cap is the **lesser of 25% of weekly disposable earnings or the amount by which weekly pay exceeds $217.50** (30× the federal minimum wage).
- "Disposable earnings" means pay after required deductions — not after voluntary ones like 401(k) contributions.
- **Child support/alimony** can reach 50–65%; **federal debts** (including defaulted federal student loans) can be garnished without a court order at generally up to 15%.
- States can add protection beyond the federal cap — some shield most wages.
- An employer can't fire you over **one** garnishment, but can over two or more.
- Responding to a summons matters: ignoring one can produce a default judgment, which is what enables garnishment.
