## Being sued over a debt is frightening — and it's a process, not a verdict

Few pieces of mail land harder than a lawsuit from a debt collector. The papers look official, the language is formal, and the first reaction is usually a knot in the stomach: what happens to me now?

Start here: a lawsuit is the **beginning** of a process, not the end of one. It's a claim a collector is making in court — and the road from being served to any actual outcome runs through deadlines and decision points where you still have choices. The worst positions in a debt collection case usually come from the step that gets skipped: not responding at all. Knowing the sequence — summons → response → judgment (maybe) → collection tools (maybe) — means facing the process with facts instead of fear. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))

## You've been served: what a summons actually is

A debt collection lawsuit typically starts with two documents: a **summons** and a **complaint**. The complaint lays out the collector's claim — who they say you owe, how much, and why. The summons tells you a case has been filed and starts the clock on your chance to respond.

Three things worth knowing right away:

- **Being served is not a decision.** It means a case has been filed. The court hasn't ruled on anything yet — that happens through the process, not at the mailbox.
- **The papers themselves matter.** They're the official record of the case, and they carry (or point to) the deadlines that count.
- **The complaint is a set of claims.** Some suits are valid, and the process — not panic — is where that gets tested.

## The deadline that matters most (and why missing it is expensive)

Courts require a **written response** to a summons by a deadline, and that deadline is set by **state law** — it varies depending on where you live. There is no single federal deadline for answering a civil summons; your summons and your state's court rules are what tell you the date that applies to you. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))

The deadline matters because of what happens when it passes unanswered: the court can enter a **default judgment** — a decision for the collector made because no one contested the claim. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs)) A default judgment isn't a technicality. It's the step that opens the door to the collection tools described below.

The point of responding isn't that you must win. It's that **responding keeps you in the process** — a default takes your voice out of the case entirely.

One honest note: this article is educational and isn't legal advice, and it can't tell you what your specific deadline is or what your response should say. If you've been served, a **local legal aid office or a licensed attorney in your state** is the right next step.

## What a judgment lets them do

If the case ends in a judgment for the collector — by default or otherwise — the judgment is what enables the post-judgment collection tools. A judgment doesn't move money by itself; it gives the collector legal tools it can then pursue. Two examples:

- **Wage garnishment.** With a judgment, a collector can generally seek an order to have a portion of your paycheck withheld. Federal law caps what can be taken for most consumer debts at the **lesser** of 25% of your weekly disposable earnings or the amount by which your weekly disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage — and some states go further in protecting pay. The full rules, including which debts follow different rules, are in [our wage garnishment guide](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/). (15 U.S.C. §1673)
- **Bank levies.** Depending on your state's procedures, a judgment can also be used to pursue money in a bank account. Levies have their own rules and exemptions, which vary by state.

What a creditor can actually do with a judgment — and how each tool gets started — is governed by state procedures on top of the federal rules above. The takeaway isn't that a judgment makes everything automatic; it's that a judgment is the legal doorway those tools stand behind. Which is exactly why the response deadline in the previous section matters: staying in the process is how you keep that doorway from opening by default.

## Your realistic options: dispute, negotiate, or get help

Nowhere in a debt collection case are your options wider than before a judgment exists. A few, framed honestly:

- **Respond through the court process.** Courts require a written response by the state-set deadline (see above). What a response can include — and what makes sense in your case — is exactly the question to take to legal aid or an attorney; this article doesn't give that advice.
- **Check the debt's details.** Debt collectors generally must send a written **validation notice** within five days of first contact, stating the amount owed and the creditor's name, and noting that the debt will be assumed valid unless you dispute it in writing within 30 days. (15 U.S.C. §1692g(a)) If the numbers or the creditor don't look right to you, that's something to raise — with the collector in writing, and with legal aid or an attorney about your options.
- **Consider settling.** Reaching an agreement to resolve the debt — a lump sum or a payment plan — is one of the realistic paths, and it's usually strongest before a judgment attaches. Outcomes vary case by case; nothing here promises what a collector will accept.

There's an old piece of wisdom that maps straight onto this. Jesus described a defendant who, while "on the way" to court with an accuser, should "make an effort to settle with him" before the case reaches the judge (Luke 12:58, ESV). The point was about reconciling early rather than letting a dispute harden into something worse — and the practical translation for a debt suit is the same: engaging while your leverage is intact beats waiting until a default judgment has taken the options off the table.

- **Get help.** A local legal aid office or a licensed consumer attorney in your state can look at your actual paperwork and deadline. FDCPA violations can support claims against a collector, but outcomes vary — no one can promise a result, and this article won't try.

## Is the debt even collectible? Statutes of limitations and time-barred suits

Every state sets a **statute of limitations** — a time limit on how long a creditor or collector can sue to enforce a debt through the courts. How long the clock runs, and what restarts it, varies by state and by debt type.

When that limit has passed, a debt is often called **time-barred**. Collecting one by lawsuit is complicated territory: the FDCPA prohibits false or misleading representations in collecting a debt (15 U.S.C. §1692e), and where the law treats a suit on a time-barred debt as misleading, bringing that suit **may** violate the FDCPA. Court treatment varies, though — it isn't a flat rule — so if you think the debt you're being sued over may be past its statute of limitations, that's a question to take to legal aid or an attorney who knows your state's law.

## The FDCPA doesn't pause for lawsuits

A lawsuit changes the stakes, but it doesn't suspend the rules of collector conduct:

- **No false or misleading representations.** The FDCPA prohibits misrepresenting the legal character of the debt or of any legal action — the same provision that's the basis for challenges to time-barred suits above. (15 U.S.C. §1692e)
- **Validation rights from the start.** The five-day written validation notice and the 30-day written dispute window (15 U.S.C. §1692g(a)) apply to contact with you — and collectors may use a standard form for it: CFPB's Regulation F prescribes the content of the Model Validation Notice. (12 CFR §1006.34)

For the fuller picture of what collectors can and can't do in day-to-day contact — call rules, what they must tell you, how to dispute — see [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/).

## The short version

- A debt collection suit starts with a **summons and complaint** — that's a case being filed, not a court decision.
- Courts require a **written response** by a deadline set by **state law**; there's no single federal deadline. Missing it can produce a **default judgment**.
- A **judgment** is what enables post-judgment tools: wage garnishment (federal cap for most consumer debts: the lesser of 25% of weekly disposable earnings or disposable earnings above 30× the federal minimum wage) and, depending on state procedure, bank levies.
- Collectors generally must send a written **validation notice within 5 days** of first contact; the debt is assumed valid unless you dispute **in writing within 30 days**. (15 U.S.C. §1692g(a))
- Statutes of limitations are state-set; suing on a time-barred debt **may** violate the FDCPA where the law treats the suit as misleading — treatment varies.
- The FDCPA still applies during a lawsuit — no false or misleading representations, including about the legal character of the debt or action. (15 U.S.C. §1692e)
- Responding keeps you in the process. A local legal aid office or a licensed attorney in your state is the right next step for your specific case.
