## You've got a few debts, you're paying the minimums, and there's a little extra left over each month. Now what? Which debt gets the extra — and does it even matter?

People argue about this constantly, and the arguments usually come down to three named methods: the **debt snowball**, the **debt avalanche**, and the **cash-flow method**. Here's what each one actually is, what it's good at, and where it falls short — so you can pick an order and get moving.

### Why the order you pay debts in matters

First, the part all three methods share: pay the **minimum on every debt**, then put whatever extra you have toward **one debt at a time**. When that one is gone, its payment rolls into the next target. The methods don't disagree about the mechanic — only about which debt should get the extra money first. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/); [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/topics/debt))

One more thing worth knowing before the showdown: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance is that **any consistent plan beats no plan**. When you're choosing an order, the factors that matter include interest rates, balances, and whether a debt has special consequences — things like secured debt, taxes, or utilities, which can jump the queue regardless of which method you follow. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/))

### The debt snowball: small wins first

The snowball method says: list your debts from **smallest balance to largest**, pay minimums on everything, and throw all your extra money at the smallest one. When it's paid off, roll that entire payment into the next-smallest debt, and keep going. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/))

The appeal is psychological, and it's real: you get a fully paid-off debt quickly, and quick wins are motivating. Watching one account disappear is what keeps a lot of people going.

The tradeoff is just as real: the snowball generally isn't the cheapest way to pay off debt. If your smallest balances carry low rates while a big balance sits there accruing interest at 25%, you'll likely pay more in total interest than you needed to.

### The debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

The avalanche method says: put your extra money toward the debt with the **highest interest rate** first, regardless of balance. This is the math-optimal ordering — paying the highest-rate debt first **minimizes the total interest you pay over time**. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/))

The tradeoff is motivation. If your highest-rate debt is also your biggest one, it can take a long time to see any account disappear. A payoff plan you abandon in month three saves you nothing — which is exactly why the snowball exists.

### The cash-flow method: free up your monthly budget

The third approach targets something different: your monthly budget. The idea is to pay off debts with the **highest minimum payment relative to their balance** first, so each payoff frees up the most monthly cash flow the fastest. The common version uses a "cash flow index" — balance divided by minimum payment — with the highest ratio paid off first.

Two honest notes on this one:

- It's a **third-party framework, not government guidance**. The cash flow index was popularized by Garrett B. Gunderson's book *Killing Sacred Cows*. The CFPB does talk about freeing up room in your monthly budget as a debt-reduction goal, but it doesn't endorse this specific ordering formula. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/))
- Its blind spot is interest rates. It doesn't consider them at all, so it will usually cost more in total interest than the avalanche. What you're buying with that extra cost is **breathing room sooner** — a smaller required monthly outlay, which matters a lot if your income is tight or irregular.

### Which one should you pick?

Here's the honest version, without declaring a winner:

- **Avalanche** is the choice that minimizes total interest paid.
- **Snowball** trades some of that math for motivation and visible progress.
- **Cash flow** trades more math for monthly breathing room, fastest.

No method is universally "best" — how long any of them takes depends entirely on your income, your rates, and your balances, so be wary of anyone promising you a debt-free date. And the CFPB's own framing is worth repeating: a plan you follow consistently beats a perfect plan you don't. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-best-way-to-pay-off-debt-en-1447/))

A practical way to choose: pick the one whose tradeoff you can live with, and start. You can always adjust later — some people clear one small, annoying debt first for the win, then switch to avalanche for the math.

### When to get help: credit counseling basics

If you can't reliably cover even the minimums, that's a sign to bring in help. A **nonprofit credit counseling agency** can review your full budget and help you build a payoff plan; that kind of budget and credit advice is typically free or low-cost. Be aware that agencies may charge fees for other services, such as setting up and managing a debt management plan — ask for the complete fee schedule in writing before you enroll in anything. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/topics/debt))
