## A collector called — now what?

The first call from a debt collector tends to produce one of two reactions: pay immediately to make it go away, or dodge the calls and hope the problem goes away. Neither is the best move. The most valuable thing you can do in the first 30 days after a collector contacts you is to neither pay nor dodge — it's to make the collector show its work.

The FDCPA — the federal law that spells out what collectors can and can't do, and the subject of [our guide](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/) — gives you a set of rights from the first contact, and one of them is built for exactly this moment: the right to demand validation of the debt. A validation letter is free, doesn't require a lawyer, and is your first line of defense against paying a debt that's wrong, inflated, or too old to enforce. This article walks through what the collector owes you first, what happens when you dispute in writing, and what a validation letter can — and can't — do.

## The validation notice: what the collector must send you

Before you send anything, the collector owes you paper. Within five days of first contacting you, a debt collector must send a written notice stating the amount of the debt, the name of the creditor to whom it's owed, and a statement that the collector will assume the debt is valid unless you dispute it in writing within 30 days. That requirement comes from 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(a), and it's not a courtesy — it's the law.

The notice has also been modernized. Regulation F, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's debt-collection rule, defines exactly what the notice must contain — the rule calls it the "validation information" — and provides a model form collectors can follow (12 C.F.R. §§ 1006.34, 1006.38). The point of all this machinery is that you shouldn't have to guess what's being alleged: the amount, the creditor, and the dispute deadline are supposed to arrive in writing.

Two things to keep in mind when the notice lands. First, **a notice is a claim, not proof** — it tells you what the collector believes and what it will assume if you stay silent. Second, **the 30-day window in that statement is real**, and it's the hinge for everything in the next section.

## How to dispute in writing — and what it actually does

If you dispute the debt in writing within that 30-day window, the law shifts in your favor in one concrete way: the collector must stop collection of the debt until it obtains verification of the debt and mails that verification to you (15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b)).

A few practical notes on how this plays out:

- **It has to be in writing, and it has to land inside the window.** A phone dispute doesn't trigger the pause. The CFPB publishes free sample letters you can adapt, including one that disputes a debt and asks for validation — you don't need to draft legal language from scratch.
- **The pause is a pause, not an eraser.** Disputing stops collection of the disputed debt while the collector verifies it — it doesn't erase the debt, and it isn't permanent. Once the collector has verified the debt and mailed you the verification, collection can resume. Regulation F separately limits how often collectors may call; a dispute letter is not a stop-all-calls order.
- **Silence has a consequence.** If you don't dispute in writing within 30 days, the collector may go on treating the debt as valid — that's the assumption the notice itself warned you about.

## What a validation letter can and can't do

This is the section worth reading twice, because the validation letter gets oversold in both directions.

**What it does:** it forces the collector to show its work. A validation letter proves nothing about who owes what — it makes the collector demonstrate what its claim is based on. Before you pay anyone anything, you should know what the debt is, who the original creditor was, and whether the collector can back up the number.

**What it doesn't do:**

- **It doesn't erase the debt.** A dispute is not a discharge, and verification is not an admission that the collector's claim is wrong.
- **It doesn't clean up your credit report by itself.** Correcting or removing an entry runs through a separate dispute process under the Fair Credit Reporting Act — a different letter, aimed at the credit bureaus rather than the collector.
- **It doesn't stop a lawsuit.** A collector that verifies the debt can still sue. If that happens, responding to the summons is covered in [Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/) — and a judgment that follows can reach beyond the lawsuit itself, including onto your home ([Judgment Lien on Your House: What It Means and What a Creditor Can Actually Do](/resources/article/judgment-lien-on-your-house/)).
- **It doesn't change what a charged-off debt means.** If the debt shows as "charged off" on your credit report, that's an accounting entry, not a cancellation — you may still owe it. [What Does "Charged Off" Mean on Your Credit Report? (And Do You Still Owe the Debt?)](/resources/article/what-does-charge-off-mean/) untangles that.

There's counsel older than any of these statutes that fits the moment: "If one gives an answer before he hears, it is his folly and shame" (Proverbs 18:13, ESV). A validation letter is that discipline in letter form — hear the full account before you answer with money.

## Time-barred debt: why the letter beats a payment

If the debt the collector is calling about is old, one more thing matters before any money changes hands: a payment can have legal consequences you can't take back. The FTC's consumer guidance warns that making a payment on a time-barred debt — a debt past the statute of limitations for a lawsuit — can restart the clock in some states. The validation letter is the place to ask the question first: what is this debt, who is it owed to, and is it still enforceable? A letter asks those questions; a payment can change your legal position.

One honest caveat in the other direction: disputing an old debt isn't automatically without risk either — a dispute can invite a lawsuit in some situations. The tradeoffs, and the difference between the clock on suing you and the clock on your credit report, are covered in [Time-Barred Debt: How Long Can a Debt Collector Come After You?](/resources/article/time-barred-debt/). The lengths involved vary by state and by debt type; you shouldn't take a collector's word for where your clock stands.

## Sample letters and where to get help

You don't have to compose a validation letter from scratch:

- **CFPB sample letters.** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau publishes free, ready-to-use sample letters for debt-collection situations, including one that disputes a debt and requests validation. Adapt it to your facts, send it in writing, and keep a copy of everything you send and receive.
- **FTC Debt Collection FAQs.** Plain-language answers on disputing debts, partial payments, and the risks around old debts.
- **If the collector breaks the rules,** you can file a complaint with the CFPB or the FTC.
- **If a lawsuit is already underway,** you're past the letter stage — the response deadline is the urgent thing, and a licensed attorney or a local legal aid office can help you answer it.

## The short version

- Within five days of first contact, a collector must send a written **validation notice**: the amount of the debt, the creditor's name, and a statement that the debt will be assumed valid unless you dispute in writing within 30 days (15 U.S.C. § 1692g(a); Regulation F defines the required "validation information" and provides a model form).
- **Dispute in writing within 30 days** and the collector must stop collection of the disputed debt until it obtains verification and mails it to you (§ 1692g(b)). The CFPB publishes free sample letters you can adapt.
- The pause is **temporary and limited** — it ends when verification is mailed, and it doesn't erase the debt, clean your credit report by itself, or stop a lawsuit.
- A validation letter **proves nothing about who owes — it forces the collector to show its work.**
- On an **old debt, the letter beats a payment**: paying a time-barred debt can restart the statute of limitations in some states. Ask first — and know that disputing an old debt can invite a suit.
- Keep copies of everything you send and receive, and act inside the 30-day window.
