## Missing a payment starts a timeline, not an avalanche

If you've missed a credit card payment — or you're staring at a due date knowing the money isn't there — the fear usually compresses into a single question: *what happens now?* The honest answer is reassuring in a specific way: the consequences arrive in stages, on a fairly predictable timeline, and every stage has more options in it than panic suggests. This article walks that timeline month by month — what happens at each point, what's law versus industry practice, and what you can still do along the way.

Two things before the timeline starts. First, nothing here is a suggestion to stop paying as a negotiating tactic — this is a map of consequences, not a strategy guide, and it's written for people who missed payments for reasons that had nothing to do with strategy: a job loss, a medical bill, an ordinary bad month. Second, if you can still act *before* a payment is missed, that's the cheapest moment to act, and the last sections explain why.

## The first missed payment: a late fee, not a catastrophe

Card issuers have to deliver your statement at least 21 days before the payment due date (12 C.F.R. § 1026.5(b)(2)(ii)(A)) — a federal rule that guarantees a real window between seeing the bill and owing it.

If the due date passes without a payment, the first consequences are small and immediate rather than dramatic:

- **A late fee.** Federal rules cap how large card late fees can be, and the caps have been in flux in recent years, so the reliable place to find your number is your card agreement — not a headline.
- **Interest keeps running.** The balance doesn't pause, and a missed payment can end promotional rate treatment your agreement provides for.
- **Contact from the issuer.** Most issuers reach out within days. It's unpleasant, and it's also the earliest, cheapest moment to call back and ask what arrangements exist.

What does *not* happen in the first two weeks: a credit-report hit. Credit bureaus generally don't see a late payment until an account is around 30 days past due — industry practice, not a statutory deadline. One forgotten due date caught and paid quickly often never reaches your credit report at all.

## Around 30 days: the credit report

Once a payment is roughly 30 days late, the late payment generally shows up on your credit reports. The "generally" is doing real work — the 30-day mark is bureau and issuer practice rather than a legal requirement, so exact timing varies — but you should plan as if it lands right at 30 days.

What *is* statute is how long it stays. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, delinquent information generally remains on your report for seven years from the date of the first delinquency that started the chain (15 U.S.C. § 1681c(a)(1), (c)(1)). Two details are worth pinning down:

- The seven-year clock keys to the *first* delinquency, not to each individual late payment along the way.
- This reporting clock is a different clock from the statute of limitations — the separate, state-law deadline for a collector to sue you over a debt. Readers conflate the two constantly because both get described as "seven years." [Time-Barred Debt: How Long Can a Debt Collector Come After You?](/resources/article/time-barred-debt/) covers the lawsuit clock.

## Around 60 days: the penalty APR

If a payment is more than 60 days late, Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. § 1026.55(b)(4)) permits the issuer to apply a penalty APR — and it can apply to your *existing* balance, not just future purchases. Penalty rates can run dramatically higher than your regular rate, which makes the 60-day line one of the most expensive thresholds on this entire timeline.

The same regulation builds in a way back: after you make six consecutive on-time minimum payments, the issuer must review the account and adjust the rate as appropriate. Be precise about what that is: a required review, not a guaranteed reset. This article isn't promising a date when your rate comes back down — but the review right is in the rule, and it's a concrete reason the on-time streak matters even after a penalty rate has landed.

## Between 90 and 180 days: the charge-off

After the second and third missed payments, most accounts move to internal collections and the contact gets more persistent. Then, around the six-month mark, the account generally charges off.

"Charge-off" sounds like an ending. It's an accounting event: the lender writes the debt off its books as unlikely to be collected. For open-end credit — credit cards — the tax code's write-off rules generally place that charge-off at 180 days after the most recent payment (26 U.S.C. § 6050P(c)). Installment loans often charge off sooner, around 120 days, as industry practice rather than statute. There is no universal day-count that applies to every debt, and the exact date in your case depends on your account's payment history.

What doesn't change at charge-off is the debt itself. You still owe it. It can still be reported, sold, and collected. [What Does "Charged Off" Mean on Your Credit Report? (And Do You Still Owe the Debt?)](/resources/article/what-does-charge-off-mean/) walks through the event in detail — what it means, what it doesn't, and who can collect afterward.

## After the charge-off: collectors, lawsuits, and garnishment

After a charge-off, the account often leaves the original issuer — placed with a collection agency or sold to a debt buyer — and a different set of federal rules takes over. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and Regulation F govern what third-party collectors can do: when they can call, what they must tell you, and what they can't say. [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/) is the overview; if a collector contacts you, [Debt Validation Letters: What They Are, When to Send One, and What They Actually Do](/resources/article/debt-validation-letter/) covers the first move worth making.

The consequences can also escalate past phone calls. A collector can sue over the debt, and if you're served, the response deadline matters enormously — [Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/) walks through that process. A judgment then opens collection tools that didn't exist before it: wage garnishment has its own rules and caps ([Wage Garnishment: How Much Can They Take From Your Paycheck?](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/)), and in some situations a judgment can become a lien against property you own ([Judgment Lien on Your House: What It Means and What a Creditor Can Actually Do](/resources/article/judgment-lien-on-your-house/) — a lien, notably, is not the same thing as losing the home).

## What you can still do at every stage

Every stage of this timeline has a move worth making. None of them erases what already happened; all of them shrink what happens next.

**Before you miss a payment:** call the issuer. Card issuers have hardship arrangements — temporary rate reductions, payment plans, adjusted due dates — and those arrangements are easiest to make while the account is still current.

**In the first 30 days:** if the payment is already missed but the account hasn't been reported yet, the same call still matters. Getting a payment made, or an arrangement started, before the 30-day mark can keep the late payment off your credit report entirely.

**Once the account is delinquent:** keep engaging. Validate the debt before paying anything on it (the validation-letter article above shows how), ask what hardship or settlement options exist, and get any agreement you reach in writing.

**If small-dollar loans are part of the squeeze:** a payday loan rolling over alongside a card payment changes the math. [Payday Loans: How They Work, What They Really Cost, and How to Get Out](/resources/article/payday-loans/) covers that product and its exits separately.

**On the debt itself:** if you're juggling several debts, the order you pay them in matters more than most people assume. [Snowball, Avalanche, or Cash Flow: How to Pick a Debt Payoff Order](/resources/article/snowball-avalanche-cashflow/) compares the three main methods honestly — including the case for starting with the smallest balance when motivation is the constraint.

**On the minimum-payment trap:** your statement is required to show a minimum-payment warning — how long and how much it takes to pay the balance off making only minimum payments (15 U.S.C. § 1637(i)). It's sobering reading by design, and it's the data behind a simple point: minimums keep an account current, but they barely move the balance. Paying even somewhat more than the minimum shortens every timeline in this article.

**Afterward:** a charge-off or a settled account is not a permanent credit sentence. [How to Rebuild Your Credit After Debt Settlement (What Actually Works)](/resources/article/rebuild-credit-after-settlement/) covers what actually moves a credit score once the damage is done.

**If it's bigger than one card:** several accounts, income that doesn't cover the minimums, calls from multiple collectors — that's the moment for a nonprofit credit counseling agency, which can review the whole picture with you, usually free or for a modest fee. Look for agencies on the DOJ/USTP-approved list, and be wary of anyone charging large upfront fees.

There's an old instinct underneath the practical advice: face what you owe rather than hiding from it, because obligations that grow in the dark get more expensive. Romans 13:8 (ESV) states the principle plainly — "Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law." The advice in this section stands on the federal rules and the options above, not on the verse — but the verse is a very old statement of the same idea, and the practical point is the same either way: a timeline you can see is a problem you can manage.

## What won't happen: no jail, and the debt doesn't vanish

Two fears deserve direct answers, because both are wrong in reassuring ways.

**You cannot go to jail for an unpaid credit card.** The United States has no debtors' prisons. The Supreme Court settled the principle decades ago — jailing someone because they are too poor to pay a civil debt is unconstitutional (*Williams v. Illinois*, 1970; *Tate v. Short*, 1971; *Bearden v. Georgia*, 1983). The narrow exceptions that exist involve things an unpaid credit card is not: willful contempt of a court order, willful child-support nonpayment, willful tax evasion. And if a collector threatens you with arrest over a credit card debt, the threat is itself illegal — falsely representing that nonpayment can lead to arrest violates the FDCPA (15 U.S.C. § 1692e(4)).

**The debt doesn't vanish at charge-off.** As covered above, charge-off is an accounting event. The obligation survives until it's paid, settled, discharged in bankruptcy, or rendered uncollectible by the statute of limitations — and the credit-reporting clock generally keeps running for seven years from the first delinquency. That's not said to make the hole look deeper. It's said because an honest map is the difference between a problem you're managing and a fear you're fleeing.

## The short version

- Statements must arrive at least 21 days before the due date (12 C.F.R. § 1026.5(b)(2)(ii)(A)); a missed payment costs a capped late fee — not an immediate credit hit.
- Around 30 days late, the late payment generally reaches your credit reports (practice, not statute) and generally stays for seven years from the first delinquency (15 U.S.C. § 1681c).
- More than 60 days late, a penalty APR can apply to your existing balance (12 C.F.R. § 1026.55(b)(4)); after six consecutive on-time minimum payments, the issuer must review the rate.
- Open-end credit card debt generally charges off 180 days after the most recent payment (26 U.S.C. § 6050P(c)); installment debt often charges off around 120 days in practice. Charge-off is an accounting event — the debt survives.
- After charge-off, FDCPA and Regulation F rules govern collectors; a lawsuit and judgment can open garnishment and, sometimes, a lien on property.
- At every stage there's a move: call the issuer early, validate before paying a collector, mind the minimum-payment warning (15 U.S.C. § 1637(i)), pick a payoff order deliberately, and rebuild afterward.
- No jail for ordinary card debt — and an arrest threat is itself illegal (15 U.S.C. § 1692e(4)).
