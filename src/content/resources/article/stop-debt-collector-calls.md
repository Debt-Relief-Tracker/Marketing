## Why the phone won't stop

The calls tend to start small — one or two a week — and then pick up speed. New numbers, different times of day, voicemails that never quite say what the call is about. If that's your week, you're not imagining the pattern, and you're not stuck with it either: federal law puts real limits on how a debt collector may contact you, and one of them — the right to tell a collector, in writing, to stop — exists for exactly this situation.

It helps to know who's probably calling. If the debt has moved from the original lender to a collection agency, the caller is a debt collector under the FDCPA, and the rules in this article apply to that collector. If the original creditor is still collecting on its own account, the picture is different: the FDCPA generally covers third-party debt collectors, not the original creditor (15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)), though some state laws reach further. That distinction matters most for the cease-communication letter below — a letter binds a collector; it doesn't necessarily bind the bank itself.

The vague voicemails have their own explanation. Regulation F defines a "limited-content message" — a voicemail with just enough in it to avoid disclosing the debt to anyone else who hears it (12 C.F.R. § 1006.2(j)). Collectors stay inside that safe harbor, which is why the messages say so little. The message doesn't have to control your week, though. Letting it ring doesn't shrink the debt either — the useful moves are the ones that change the collector's behavior, and the rest of this article covers the two that matter: the call limits collectors must follow and the letter that ends the contact.

## The call limits collectors must follow

- **Time of day.** A collector may not call at a time or place it knows — or should know — is inconvenient, and the law presumes that means before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. your local time (15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(1), (b)).
- **Your workplace.** If the collector knows your employer prohibits these calls, it may not contact you at work (15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(3)).
- **Your attorney.** If the collector knows you're represented by an attorney about the debt and can readily get the attorney's address, contact goes through the attorney instead of you (15 U.S.C. § 1692c(a)(2)).
- **Frequency.** Under Regulation F, more than 7 calls about a debt within 7 consecutive days — or more than one call within a week after a call that connected — creates a presumption that the collector has crossed into harassment (12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)). Calls to several numbers you provided can count toward the limit, so rotating phones isn't a workaround.

A presumption isn't a bright-line ban in every case, but it's the line regulators drew, and it gives you a fair way to describe what's happening: if your phone is ringing well past it, that's worth writing down (more on that below). These are the highlights, not the whole list — [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/) covers the full set of conduct rules.

## The cease-communication letter

The main lever is also the simplest to describe. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c), once a debt collector receives written notice that you want communication about the debt to stop, it may not contact you further — with three narrow exceptions. It may tell you its efforts are being terminated; it may tell you that a specified remedy, such as a lawsuit, may be invoked; and, where applicable, it may tell you that the remedy will be invoked. That's the whole list.

You don't have to draft the letter from scratch or format it a special way. The CFPB publishes free sample letters, including a stop-contact letter, on its debt-collection page. Send it in a way you can document — keep a copy, note the date — and don't feel obligated to explain yourself: the statute doesn't ask for a reason, a negotiation, or a warning shot first.

Two limits to know before you send it. First, the letter's force follows the FDCPA's coverage: strong against third-party collectors, limited — and state-law-dependent — against an original creditor collecting its own debt. Second, you'll see paid services offering to make the calls stop for you. The letter is free, the CFPB's samples are official, and nothing in the process requires a middleman.

There's an old piece of wisdom that fits this decision better than most advice about phone calls. Proverbs 17:14 (ESV) says, "The beginning of strife is like letting out water, so quit before the quarrel breaks out." A cease-communication letter is that kind of move: not an escalation, not a surrender — a deliberate decision to end a conflict that was feeding itself. The statute, not the verse, is what makes the letter stick. The verse just names what the letter is for.

## What stopping the calls does not do

This is the section that keeps the letter honest. Stopping contact stops contact — that's all:

- **The debt doesn't disappear.** The obligation is still there, and a collector you've silenced on the phone is still free to work the file in other ways.
- **The collector can still sue.** The § 1692c(c) exceptions exist partly for this: a collector can write to tell you a lawsuit may be coming. A suit can end in a judgment, and a judgment has consequences of its own ([Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/) covers the lawsuit itself; [Judgment Lien on Your House: What It Means and What a Creditor Can Actually Do](/resources/article/judgment-lien-on-your-house/) covers what a judgment can mean for property).
- **Credit reporting continues.** A collector who can't call you can still report the account ([What Does "Charged Off" Mean on Your Credit Report? (And Do You Still Owe the Debt?)](/resources/article/what-does-charge-off-mean/) covers what continued reporting means for your credit).
- **The revive risk is real.** Before you call a collector back or write anything that acknowledges an old debt, check where the clock stands — depending on your state, a payment or an acknowledgment can restart the statute-of-limitations clock ([Time-Barred Debt: How Long Can a Debt Collector Come After You?](/resources/article/time-barred-debt/) explains the two clocks). The letter that stops the phone calls shouldn't be the thing that revives the statute-of-limitations clock.

## When calls cross into harassment

Frequency isn't the only limit. 15 U.S.C. § 1692d(5) prohibits calling with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass — a rule that stands on its own, so a collector can violate it without ever reaching the 7-in-7 line. If the calls feel like harassment rather than persistence, the practical step is the same either way: write it down. Dates, times, numbers called, what was said, whether messages were left. A log turns a feeling into a record, and a record is what a complaint — and an attorney, if it comes to that — asks for first.

The escalation paths are free: the CFPB and the FTC both accept complaints about debt-collection practices. You don't need a lawyer to file one, and the log you've kept is the main thing you'll need.

## If the debt isn't yours — or you're not sure it is

Sometimes the debt behind the calls isn't yours, or the amount looks wrong. You don't have to sort that out over the phone, and you don't have to accept the caller's version of events to make the calls stop. The validation process — disputing in writing, what the collector has to do in response, and what verification does and doesn't prove — is its own topic, covered in [Debt Validation Letters: What They Are, When to Send One, and What They Actually Do](/resources/article/debt-validation-letter/). If the calls are about a debt you genuinely don't recognize, a validation dispute may be the better first letter, because it puts the debt itself in question rather than just the phone calls.

## The short version

- A debt collector can't call whenever it wants: the 8 a.m.–9 p.m. presumption, the workplace bar where the employer prohibits calls, attorney-representation routing, and Regulation F's frequency presumptions — more than 7 calls in 7 days, or more than one after a connected call (15 U.S.C. §§ 1692b, 1692c; 12 C.F.R. § 1006.14(b)(2)).
- The cease-communication letter is the main lever: written notice under 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c) stops further contact, except three narrow notices (efforts ended; a remedy may be invoked; a remedy will be invoked).
- Use the CFPB's free sample letters — keep a copy and note the date you sent it.
- The letter stops contact, not the debt: the collector can still sue and can still report.
- Check where the statute of limitations stands before writing anything that acknowledges an old debt — an acknowledgment can restart the statute-of-limitations clock depending on your state.
- Harassment (15 U.S.C. § 1692d(5)) is its own violation: document the calls and use the CFPB or FTC complaint routes.
- The FDCPA covers third-party collectors, not generally the original creditor — and if the debt isn't yours, a validation dispute may be the better first letter.
