## The short answer

If you've lost track of what you owe, the list still exists — it's just scattered. Some of it sits on your credit reports, some of it is in a drawer of unopened mail, and some of it lives in an online account you stopped checking months ago. This article walks through where to look and in what order: your free credit reports first, then the mail, then what to do when a collector calls about a debt you don't recognize. The goal is a written inventory, because you can't pay off, settle, or dispute a debt you haven't actually named.

## Start with what you can see: your free credit reports

The place to start is your credit reports. [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/index.action) is the official source for free reports from all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Reports from each bureau are free weekly under a program the bureaus made permanent. That sits on top of the right you already have under federal law: the Fair Credit Reporting Act entitles every consumer to their file from each bureau (15 U.S.C. § 1681g).

Pull from all three, not just one. The bureaus don't receive identical information from creditors, so an account that appears on one report can be missing from another. One report is a partial picture on its own; three, compared side by side, get you much closer to the whole thing.

## What the report shows — and what it can miss

A credit report lists open and closed accounts, your payment history on each, and any accounts that have gone to collections. A debt in collections shows up on the report as its own entry — called a tradeline. [What Does "Charged Off" Mean on Your Credit Report? (And Do You Still Owe the Debt?)](/resources/article/what-does-charge-off-mean/) explains what those entries mean and how long they stay.

Two gaps are worth knowing about before you treat the report as the complete list:

- **Not every creditor reports to every bureau.** Reporting is voluntary for creditors, and coverage varies by creditor and by bureau. A debt that appears nowhere on your reports may still be owed.
- **Some debts only surface once they're in collections.** A past-due utility bill is one example of a debt that may not show up until it's in collections. [Can't Pay Your Electric Bill? Utility Shutoff Rules, Protections, and Where to Get Help](/resources/article/utility-shutoff/) covers how that works.

So treat the reports as the best starting inventory you have, not the complete one. They're where the trail starts, not where it ends.

## Mine the mail: validation notices and old statements

The second source is probably already in your house. When a collector first contacts you about a debt, federal law requires them to provide validation information — the amount of the debt claimed and the name of the creditor to whom it's owed — at or shortly after that first contact (15 U.S.C. § 1692g; the detailed content requirements sit in Regulation F, 12 C.F.R. § 1006.34). If you've been avoiding the mailbox, the letters already in it are evidence: each one names an amount and a creditor.

Old account statements do the same work from the other direction — a card statement from last year shows the balance as the issuer saw it. And if the original creditor still exists, its online portal or statement archive can show the balance.

If a letter looks like a collections notice, keep it. [Debt Validation Letters: What They Are, When to Send One, and What They Actually Do](/resources/article/debt-validation-letter/) explains what collectors are required to tell you and what the letter in your hand entitles you to ask for.

## When a collector calls about a debt you don't recognize

Sometimes the list finds you first: the phone rings, and the person on the other end says you owe money you can't place. The Federal Trade Commission's advice for that call is to get information before you give any — ask for the collector's name and address and the amount they say you owe, and don't pay anything on the spot.

You don't have to resolve the call in the moment. The rules that govern it are federal: the FDCPA sets out what a collector may and may not do, and [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/) is the full rundown. Once the call ends, the same validation step applies as with any letter — confirm the debt is real and that the people calling have the right to collect it before money changes hands. And if the calls keep coming, [How to Stop Debt Collector Calls: Your Rights and the Steps That Actually Work](/resources/article/stop-debt-collector-calls/) covers the written letter that makes contact stop.

## What checking your own report doesn't cost you

One thing stops a lot of people from looking: the worry that checking will hurt their credit. A report you pull yourself is a soft inquiry, and checking your own report doesn't hurt your credit — that's the CFPB's guidance.

So the cost of looking is roughly an hour. The cost of not looking is that the deadlines attached to each debt — to dispute, to respond, to act — keep running whether or not you've seen them.

## From list to plan: validate, prioritize, and don't let deadlines run

Once the list exists, it becomes workable. A written inventory turns a vague cloud of worry into a set of specific items, each with an amount, a holder, and a status. There's an old line about counting the cost before you build: Luke 14:28 (ESV) records Jesus asking, "For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost whether he has enough to complete it?" The inventory is that counting. Whatever a reader makes of the source, the practical point holds on its own — you can't dig out of what you haven't written down.

Three moves turn the list into a plan:

- **Validate each entry.** For anything in collections, the validation letter covered above is the first move — confirm what the debt is and who holds it before paying anything on it.
- **Prioritize by what each debt can actually do.** The order isn't only about balances. [Judgment Lien on Your House: What It Means and What a Creditor Can Actually Do](/resources/article/judgment-lien-on-your-house/) covers one piece of that: what a lien is — and what it isn't.
- **Pick an order and start.** Once you know the balances, a payoff method gives you the sequence. [Snowball, Avalanche, or Cash Flow: How to Pick a Debt Payoff Order](/resources/article/snowball-avalanche-cashflow/) compares the main approaches. And if the list includes debts that were already settled or charged off, [How to Rebuild Your Credit After Debt Settlement (What Actually Works)](/resources/article/rebuild-credit-after-settlement/) covers what happens to your reports from there.

Whatever order you pick, handle the deadline items first. A summons or a dispute window is a date on a calendar, and it doesn't move because the debt was out of sight.

## The honest limits

- **Your credit report is the best starting inventory, not the complete one.** Reporting coverage varies by creditor and bureau, and some debts only appear once they're in collections.
- **Finding a debt doesn't settle it, and ignoring it doesn't erase it.** The balance stays owed until it's paid or settled — and the deadlines attached to it run whether or not you look.
- **Errors and fraud have their own paths.** If something on your reports isn't yours or isn't accurate, there are dispute processes through the credit bureaus and specific steps for identity theft. Both are their own topics; this article doesn't try to cover them.
- **No reliable figure exists** for how many consumers carry debt in collections, and this article doesn't assert one. What's knowable is the process — and that's what's covered here.

## The short version

- Free weekly reports from all three bureaus are available at AnnualCreditReport.com — pull all three, because they don't match.
- A report shows open and closed accounts and any collection tradelines, but not every creditor reports to every bureau — a missing account isn't proof a debt is gone.
- The mail is evidence: validation notices and old statements name amounts and creditors.
- If a collector calls about a debt you don't recognize, get their name, address, and the claimed amount before paying anything — then validate.
- Checking your own report doesn't hurt your credit — a self-pull is a soft inquiry.
- From the list: validate each debt, weigh what each one can actually do, pick a payoff order, and handle the deadline items first.
