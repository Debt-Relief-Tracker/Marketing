## Two different clocks: the credit report vs. the courthouse

Finding an old debt — on a credit report, in a stack of mail, in a collector's phone message — tends to raise the same question: after all this time, can they still come after me? Can a debt collector sue me after 7 years?

The honest answer starts with a distinction most people were never told about. **Two different clocks** govern an old debt, and they run independently:

- **The credit-reporting clock.** Federal law limits how long most negative information — including collection accounts — can stay on your credit report, generally around seven years. That clock decides what appears on your report. It does not decide whether the debt can still be enforced in court.
- **The lawsuit clock.** Every state sets a **statute of limitations** — a time limit on how long a creditor or debt collector can sue you over a debt. How long it runs, and exactly when it starts, depends on your state and the type of debt. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))

Neither clock controls the other, and they frequently disagree. A debt can be past the statute of limitations and still sitting on your credit report. It can also drop off your credit report and still be inside the lawsuit window. Mixing the two up is where costly mistakes come from — either assuming a debt is uncollectible when it isn't, or assuming it's still fully collectible when the law may say otherwise.

This article is about the second clock: the courthouse one — the statute of limitations on debt.

## What "time-barred" means (and what it doesn't)

When the statute of limitations on debt has run out, the debt is often called **time-barred**. Here's what that does and doesn't mean.

**What it means:** under your state's law, the window for a creditor or collector to win a lawsuit over the debt has closed. If you're sued anyway, an expired statute of limitations is a **defense** the court can consider — more on how that works below. The purpose of these limits is practical, not personal: as time passes, records scatter and memories blur, and courts don't want to decide stale claims where the evidence has fallen apart.

**What it doesn't mean:**

- **The debt isn't erased.** Time-barred describes your exposure to a lawsuit, not the debt's existence.
- **It doesn't remove anything from your credit report.** The reporting clock is separate (see above).
- **Collectors aren't banned from contacting you.** They may still reach out about a time-barred debt and ask you to pay it — voluntarily. That's legal. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))

There's an old precedent for the idea that a debt shouldn't hang over a person forever. Ancient Israel's law instructed creditors, "At the end of every seven years you shall grant a release" — a scheduled letting-go of debts the text calls "the LORD's release" (Deuteronomy 15:1–2, ESV). A statute of limitations works differently — it exists to keep courts from deciding stale claims — but the instinct underneath is one our legal system still carries: the law itself draws a line past which a creditor's claim goes quiet. The practical takeaway is the same whichever way you come at it: old debt occupies a different legal position than fresh debt, and it's worth knowing which one you're dealing with.

## The reset trap: payments and acknowledgments that restart the clock

Here's the part that surprises people most: the statute of limitations clock can **restart**.

In some states, taking certain actions on an old debt — making a payment, promising to pay, or acknowledging that the debt is yours, sometimes just in writing — can restart the clock, in some cases back to the full original limitation period. States differ on which actions count and by how much. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/); [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))

That's worth sitting with, because it changes the calculus on an old debt:

- A small goodwill payment on a debt you believed was behind you can, in some states, make the whole amount newly sueable.
- A reply to a collector that acknowledges the debt can have the same effect in some states.

This is why the blunt advice you'll sometimes see — "never pay an old debt," "never acknowledge one" — is too blunt to be useful. Whether paying or acknowledging is risky in your case depends on your state's law, where your clock stands, and what actually happened on the account. Those are exactly the details worth checking — with your state's official resources, or with a consumer attorney or local legal aid office — **before** you pay anything or put anything in writing.

This article can't tell you what your state's law says or where your clock reads. That's a facts-and-jurisdiction question, and it deserves a real answer, not a guess.

## Can they still call? Contact, voluntary payment, and the FDCPA line

Yes — and it's a common misunderstanding about old debt. A collector can contact you about a time-barred debt, and asking you to pay it voluntarily is legal. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/)) These resurfacing old debts are sometimes called "zombie debt." The name is dramatic; the underlying position is ordinary. The debt exists, the collector may ask, and your protection comes from knowing where the legal lines sit.

Those lines:

- **Suing — or threatening to sue — on a time-barred debt can violate federal law.** The FDCPA prohibits debt collectors from threatening action that cannot legally be taken and from making false or misleading representations about the debt's legal character (15 U.S.C. §1692e(5), §1692f). Where the law treats a lawsuit on a time-barred debt as one of those violations, bringing the suit — or threatening one — can violate the FDCPA. Court treatment varies case by case, so if a collector sues or threatens to sue over an old debt, that's a specific question for an attorney or legal aid, not something this article can resolve.
- **The written validation notice is supposed to tell you where you stand.** More on that in the last section below.
- **If a collector crosses the line**, document the contact and raise it. FDCPA violations can support claims against a collector — though outcomes vary, and no one can promise a result. The full picture of your day-to-day rights with any collector — call rules, what they must tell you, how to dispute — is in [What Debt Collectors Can and Can't Do: Your Rights Under the FDCPA](/resources/article/fdcpa-collector-rights/).

## If you're sued on old debt: respond, and know the defense exists

If a collector sues you over an old debt, the most important step is the same as for any debt lawsuit: **respond**.

- A lawsuit starts with a **summons** and a complaint, and courts require a written response by a deadline set by state law. Missing that deadline can produce a **default judgment** — a decision for the collector because no one contested the claim. ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))
- A default judgment matters because it's what reopens the collector's full toolkit. With a judgment, a collector can generally pursue tools like wage garnishment (federal law caps what can be taken for most consumer debts) and, depending on your state's procedures, levies against a bank account.
- An expired statute of limitations is a **defense** — but it's generally one that has to be raised. It's decided by a judge in the case; it doesn't apply itself. Ignoring the suit can hand a collector a judgment without the court ever hearing the time-barred defense. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/))

We walk through the whole process — the summons, the response deadline, what a judgment allows — in [Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/), and the post-judgment tools in [our wage garnishment guide](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/).

One honest note: this article is educational and isn't legal advice. What to file in a response, and whether the statute of limitations applies to your debt, are questions for a licensed attorney in your state or a local legal aid office — especially in a live lawsuit, where the deadline is real.

## How to check your state's statute of limitations (and why the validation notice helps)

Because the statute of limitations on debt is state-set and debt-type-specific, the number that matters lives in your state's law, not in a national chart. A few reliable ways to find where you stand:

- **Your state's official consumer resources.** State attorney general consumer-protection sites and state court self-help centers publish guidance on limitation periods for common debt types. Start there rather than with a generic list — states vary, and the variations matter.
- **The debt's own paperwork.** The clock usually runs from the last payment on the account or from when the account first went delinquent — which one depends on the state. Account statements and your credit report can help pin that date down. ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/debt-collection/); [FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/debt-collection-faqs))
- **The validation notice.** Under the CFPB's Regulation F, a collector's validation notice must state — in plain terms — whether the law limits how long the collector can sue you for the debt (12 CFR §1006.34, with model language in Appendix B-1). In other words, the paperwork is designed to tell you the suit-limitation status up front. If a collector has contacted you, read that notice before anything else.
- **A professional read.** A consumer attorney or a local legal aid office can apply your state's law to your actual facts. That's how to get an answer specific to your facts — and it's the right step if a lawsuit is already underway.

What you're looking for is the limitation period for your debt's type in your state, plus the date the clock started. Those two facts — not a rule of thumb — determine whether a debt is time-barred for you.

## The short version

- An old debt is governed by **two independent clocks**: the credit-reporting limit (federal law, generally around seven years for most negative items) and the **statute of limitations** on lawsuits (state law, varies by debt type). Neither controls the other.
- **Time-barred** means the lawsuit window has closed under your state's law. It doesn't erase the debt, doesn't remove it from your credit report, and doesn't stop collectors from contacting you — they may still ask you to pay voluntarily.
- In some states, a **payment, a promise to pay, or an acknowledgment** of the debt can restart the clock — sometimes back to the full original period. Check your state's law before paying or acknowledging an old debt.
- Suing — or threatening to sue — on a time-barred debt **can violate the FDCPA** (15 U.S.C. §1692e(5), §1692f); courts treat this case by case.
- The **validation notice** must state whether the law limits how long the collector can sue you. (12 CFR §1006.34)
- If you're sued on an old debt, **respond**: a default judgment restores collection powers, and an expired statute of limitations is a defense that has to be raised for a judge to decide it.
- For your specific case, a **consumer attorney or local legal aid office** is the right next step.
