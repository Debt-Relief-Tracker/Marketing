## What a judgment lien is — and how one gets attached to your home

If you've been sued over a debt and lost — or didn't respond and a default judgment was entered — you already know a judgment is a court's determination that you owe the money. What many people don't realize is what a judgment lets a creditor do next. A judgment can reach your paycheck — that's covered in [our wage garnishment guide](/resources/article/wage-garnishment/) — and one of its most consequential tools is the **judgment lien on real estate**.

The mechanics vary by state, but the pattern is similar: once a creditor holds a money judgment, recording it — an abstract of the judgment filed with the county recorder, or docketing it in the state's judgment system — generally creates a lien on the debtor's real property in that county or jurisdiction. New York, for example, makes a docketed judgment a lien on real property under CPLR § 5203; California (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. §§ 697.310–697.340) and Texas (Tex. Prop. Code ch. 52) use recorded abstracts of judgment. Those three are examples of mechanisms, not a rule for your state — the exact trigger, coverage, and paperwork in your state may differ, so verify against your state's statutes or court self-help resources.

Two things worth noting at the outset. First, a judgment lien usually attaches **without any further hearing or notice to you** — the recording itself does the work. Second, a judgment lien is a creature of state law, and this article can't tell you your state's rules; it can tell you the shape of the thing so you know what questions to ask.

## Can they take your house? What the lien does — and doesn't — allow

Here's the honest two-part answer that rarely gets said together:

**The lien attaches easily.** Once the judgment is recorded or docketed, the lien generally attaches to your real property in the county — including your home — and it clouds title for years.

**A lien is not a forced sale.** A judgment lien does not, by itself, give a creditor the power to sell your home. Whether a creditor can force a sale of a homestead turns on state exemption law. Some states — Texas is the classic example, where the constitution protects the homestead "from forced sale" except for enumerated liens — bar the forced sale of a homestead outright. Elsewhere, a homestead exemption protects a certain amount of the equity in the home, and a forced sale is only worth pursuing where equity survives the exemption. Those are examples of how strong the protections can be, not a prediction for your state.

So the accurate frame is: **the lien attaches; the sale is usually blocked.** Both halves matter. People who hear only the first half panic; people who hear only the second half ignore a lien that will still cost them money. Neither reaction helps.

There's a principle here older than any of these statutes. When Nehemiah confronted the nobles who were taking their neighbors' fields and houses as security for debt, his demanded remedy wasn't a compromise — it was restoration: "Return to them this very day their fields, their vineyards, their olive orchards, and their houses" (Nehemiah 5:11, ESV). The home was never meant to be the thing creditors strip first. Modern law's homestead protections and lien-avoidance rules are a secular echo of that instinct, and this article treats those limits as what they are — protections with a purpose, not loopholes. If you're facing a lien on your home, the point is not that you failed; it's that the law itself draws a line around the house, and you're entitled to know where it is.

## What the lien blocks: selling, refinancing, and clear title

Even where the home itself is safe from forced sale, the lien still costs you. A judgment lien clouds title: most title companies and lenders won't close a sale or a refinance with an unpaid judgment lien on the property. In practice that means the lien generally has to be paid or released before you can sell or refinance with clear title — or negotiated as part of the closing.

That's the real, everyday bite of a judgment lien: not an eviction, but a freeze on the equity. You can usually keep living in the house. What you can't easily do is sell it, refinance it, or borrow against it without dealing with the lien first.

## How long it lasts, and the three ways it ends

Judgment liens don't last forever, but they last a long time. Most states give a judgment lien a defined term — commonly in the neighborhood of a decade — and many allow the creditor to renew it before it expires. An expired, unrenewed lien loses its force. The exact term and renewal rules are state-specific; check your state's statute rather than assuming.

A lien ends in one of three ways:

1. **Satisfaction.** When the judgment is paid (or settled with the judgment creditor), the creditor files a satisfaction of judgment with the recorder, and the lien is released. If you pay or settle, make sure this step actually happens — an unsatisfied lien on a paid judgment is a paperwork problem you don't want at closing time.
2. **Expiry without renewal.** The lien runs out at the end of its statutory term if the creditor doesn't renew.
3. **Avoidance in bankruptcy.** Under 11 U.S.C. § 522(f)(1)(A), a debtor in bankruptcy can avoid — strip off — a judicial lien that impairs an exemption, including the homestead exemption. This is a bankruptcy remedy: it happens inside a bankruptcy case, not as a standalone fix. If lien avoidance is relevant to your situation, a bankruptcy attorney can tell you whether your home's equity and your state's exemptions make it available — this article isn't advice to file.

One important distinction regardless of which path applies: **the debt survives the lien's removal.** Satisfying the lien means paying or settling the judgment; avoiding the lien in bankruptcy is part of a bankruptcy case that may also address the underlying debt. Removing a lien and erasing the obligation are two different things — keeping the two clocks (the lien's life and the debt's life, including the separate statute of limitations on suing you, covered in [Time-Barred Debt](/resources/article/time-barred-debt/)) distinct is the same discipline that applies everywhere else in debt collection.

## Not the same thing: tax liens and mortgage foreclosure

Two lookalikes cause most of the confusion around judgment liens:

- **A federal tax lien is a different animal.** It arises by statute when the IRS assesses a tax balance and attaches to all your property and rights to property — not through a judgment in a lawsuit. Its rules, priorities, and release process are its own. If what you're facing is an IRS lien, the IRS's own materials are the right starting point, not this article's mechanics.
- **Mortgage foreclosure is not a judgment lien.** Your mortgage is a voluntary security lien you agreed to when you bought the home; foreclosure is the lender enforcing that agreement. A judgment lien from an unrelated debt — a credit card, a medical bill — is an involuntary lien that arrived through a lawsuit. Different origins, different rules, different remedies.

## First steps: search the county records, know your exemptions, act before the judgment lands

- **If a lawsuit is pending, respond.** The lien exists because a judgment exists. The cheapest time to prevent a judgment lien is before default judgment is entered — the summons-and-response process is covered in [Sued by a Debt Collector? What Happens Next and What You Can Do](/resources/article/debt-collection-lawsuit/).
- **Search your county records.** Judgments and abstracts are public records; a search of your county recorder's or clerk's records (many have online search) will show whether a lien has been recorded against your property.
- **Learn your state's exemptions.** Homestead protection is the single biggest variable in whether your home is at risk. Your state's exemption statutes or court self-help pages will say what's protected and how much. A local legal aid office or a licensed attorney in your state can apply them to your facts.
- **If a lien exists, find out its terms.** When it was recorded, when it expires, whether it's been renewed, and whether the underlying judgment has been satisfied. Records beat assumptions.
- **Don't transfer the house to defeat a lien.** Moving property out of your name after a judgment is on the table can be a fraudulent transfer, with consequences worse than the lien. Deal with the lien through the lawful paths above instead.

A judgment lien on your home is serious — it clouds title and can last for years — but it is not the same thing as losing the house, and the law gives homeowners real protections. Knowing which half of the story you're in is the first step to dealing with it.

## The short version

- A money judgment can become a **lien on your real property** when the creditor records or dockets it in county records — usually with no further hearing or notice. Mechanisms vary by state (examples: NY CPLR § 5203 docketing; Cal. Code Civ. Proc. §§ 697.310–697.340 and Tex. Prop. Code ch. 52 abstract recording).
- A judgment lien **does not by itself let a creditor sell your home.** Forced-sale outcomes depend on state exemption law — strong-homestead states bar forced sale outright; elsewhere a homestead exemption protects equity up to statutory amounts.
- The lien still **clouds title**: it generally must be paid or released before you can sell or refinance with clear title.
- Judgment liens **expire** — commonly around a decade, sometimes renewable — and end one of three ways: **satisfaction** (filed after payment/settlement), **expiry without renewal**, or **avoidance in bankruptcy** (11 U.S.C. § 522(f)(1)(A), a bankruptcy remedy).
- **The debt survives the lien's removal** — paying the lien means paying or settling the judgment; lien avoidance happens inside a bankruptcy case.
- **Federal tax liens and mortgage foreclosure are different animals** — statutory liens and voluntary security liens respectively, not judgment liens.
- **First steps:** respond to any pending lawsuit; search county records; learn your state's homestead exemption; check a recorded lien's terms; never transfer the home to defeat a lien.
